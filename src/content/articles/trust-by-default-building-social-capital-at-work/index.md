## Trust by default — Building social capital at work

![Photo by Andrea Tummons on Unsplash](./images/image-01.jpeg "Photo by Andrea Tummons on Unsplash")

In his book [Bowling Alone](https://www.amazon.ca/Bowling-Alone-Collapse-American-Community/dp/1982130849/), Robert D. Putnam highlights the increasing disconnect from family, work and established societal structures. The book warns that our stock of “Social Capital” (the very fabric of our connection with people around us) is plummeting.

For me, a relatable inference from the book was how little effort goes into building social capital in a workplace and our overreliance on measurements, calibration, and validation of perceived performance. In my formative years as an engineer, I subscribed to a piece of consistent advice from most of my leaders — “trust, but verify”. After becoming a people leader, I realized that the “trust, but verify” style was often trust-diminishing and decreased the chances of building a psychologically safe organization.

## Trust, but verify

When a leader expects each individual to earn their trust, it creates a transactional relationship contract that is present and valid only when there is consistent fulfillment of meeting goals, deadlines, or defined milestones. This expectation can lead to an obsession over outcomes and breeds unwillingness to take risks where needed. It’s not all gloom and doom on this approach — it does have its uses in the right situation. For example, when it comes to protecting security infrastructure, financial transaction systems etc., having consistent verification at every step is vital. In these cases, the importance of the outcome outweighs the process of relationship building.

In reality, most leaders in the tech world are not going to be in those emergency-room situations all the time. We need people leaders to take a different approach to trust, which helps build social capital in the long term.

## Trust by default

When leaders trust by default, they assume that a team member can learn, grow, and accomplish the desired outcomes by themselves or by working with others. They start with trust and invest in incrementally building social capital on an individual basis.

Trusting by default creates a nurturing yet empowering relationship that will encourage creativity, calculated risk-taking, psychological safety, and eventually increased efficiency. Building an organization’s social capital can only be done by the trust pockets you groom with every individual.

Teams that do not feel they need permission to fail take ambiguity head-on to produce outcomes that are customer delighting. When this happens consistently, you know that your organization is trusting by default and feels safe to do so on a daily basis.

## Sustaining and growing social capital

Trust plays a significant role in the social dynamics of a workplace. In her book [Trust, Inc](https://www.amazon.ca/Trust-Inc-Business-Engagement-Innovation/dp/1601632851/), Nan S Russel quotes that organizational relationship building starts with — “creating environments where people have pertinent information they need to do great work, make informed decisions, and build genuine relationships with self-alignment and behavioral integrity”.

In a world where organizations are increasingly becoming remote-first and distributed, building social capital through “trust by default” is the foundation on top of which any organizational practices can grow and scale. When leaders establish trust pockets one individual at a time and guide others as trust-practitioners to build their own trust pockets, the eventual synergy in the organization will make it an engaging and empowering place to work.
